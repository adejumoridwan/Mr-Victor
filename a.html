wilcox.test(compliance, background, alternative = "greater")  # W = 63.5, p = 0.0057

Yes — strong evidence of contamination. Both the one-sided Welch t-test (p = 0.0068) and Wilcoxon rank-sum test (p = 0.0057) reject H₀ at α = 0.05. The compliance well mean (14.87 ppb) is nearly double the background mean (8.14 ppb), and the compliance readings increase across months while background readings stay relatively stable.

(d) Normal probability plot for background wells

qqnorm(background); qqline(background)
shapiro.test(background)  # W = 0.921, p = 0.294

The Q-Q plot shows points close to the reference line with no systematic departures. The Shapiro-Wilk test gives p = 0.294, so we fail to reject normality — the combined background data are adequately modeled by a normal distribution.

(e) Difference between the two background wells

t.test(well1, well2, paired = TRUE)    # t = −2.575, p = 0.0497
t.test(well1, well2, paired = FALSE)   # t = −1.228, p = 0.248

Well 1 mean = 7.25 ppb; Well 2 mean = 9.03 ppb. The paired t-test (same months) gives p = 0.0497 — borderline significant. However, the independent t-test gives p = 0.248. The paired result is marginally significant but practically the difference is small relative to the spread, and both wells are well below the compliance well. For monitoring purposes, the wells can reasonably be pooled as background, though the small paired difference warrants noting.

Section 2 — Air Quality in New York

(a) Ozone in May vs August

may_ozone <- na.omit(airquality$Ozone[airquality$Month == 5])   # n=26, mean=23.6
aug_ozone <- na.omit(airquality$Ozone[airquality$Month == 8])   # n=26, mean=60.0

shapiro.test(may_ozone)  # p ≈ 0 (non-normal, right-skewed)
wilcox.test(may_ozone, aug_ozone)   # p = 0.0001
t.test(may_ozone, aug_ozone)        # p = 0.0002

There is a highly significant difference. August ozone (mean ≈ 60 ppb) is more than twice May ozone (mean ≈ 24 ppb). Since May ozone is non-normally distributed, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test is the most appropriate test; it gives p < 0.001. The Welch t-test agrees (p = 0.0002). We firmly reject H₀ at any conventional level. Higher summer temperatures and stronger sunlight drive photochemical ozone production in August.

Both KS and Shapiro-Wilk strongly reject normality for raw TP and chla. After log-transformation, log(chla) passes the Shapiro-Wilk test (p = 0.623). (d) Graphical comparison of NOAA vs ODNR (LakeErie2)

Overlaid density plots and a side-by-side boxplot show that ODNR samples (mean ≈ 87 µg/L) tend to have higher TP values than NOAA samples (mean ≈ 52 µg/L), and ODNR shows a wider spread with a heavier upper tail.

wilcox.test(noaa_TP, odnr_TP)   # W = 10732, p = 0.0015

Section 4 — Uranium and Total Dissolved Solids

(a) Are uranium and TDS correlated?

cor.test(hh$Uranium_ppb, hh$TDS_mgL, method = "pearson")   # r = 0.637, p < 0.001
cor.test(hh$Uranium_ppb, hh$TDS_mgL, method = "spearman")  # ρ = 0.716, p < 0.001

Yes — uranium and TDS are significantly positively correlated. Both Pearson (r = 0.637) and Spearman (ρ = 0.716) are statistically significant (p < 0.001).

(b) Strength of the relationship

The Pearson r = 0.637 indicates a moderate-to-strong positive linear relationship. The regression R² = 0.406, meaning TDS explains about 41% of the variance in uranium concentration. The Spearman ρ = 0.716 (slightly higher) suggests the monotone relationship on the original or log scale is even stronger. In environmental terms, both uranium and TDS tend to increase together in groundwater — likely driven by the same geological factors (mineral dissolution, evaporative concentration) that enrich both uranium and dissolved solids.
