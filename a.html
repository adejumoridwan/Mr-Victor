Exercise 1 — Germination Times of Phaseolus vulgaris

a. Experimental/Observation Units & Statistical Population

The experimental (sampling) units are the individual seeds. Each seed is independently assigned to one of three moisture level treatments and its germination time is recorded.

The statistical population is the conceptual collection of all Phaseolus vulgaris seeds that could potentially be germinated under the three soil moisture conditions studied. The 90 seeds (30 per group) represent a sample drawn from this population.

b. Type of Data

Germination time is a continuous numeric variable — it is measured on a ratio scale (e.g., in days or hours) and can take any positive real value within a range. It has a true zero (a seed that never germinates, or germinates immediately).

c. Dependent and Independent Variables

Role Variable
Independent variable (factor/predictor) Soil moisture level (3 levels) — categorical/nominal
Dependent variable (response) Germination time (days/hours) — continuous

The moisture level is actively manipulated (or at least categorised), making it the factor whose effect we are studying.

d. Sample Size

  • Per group: 30 seeds
  • Total sample size: n = 90 (3 groups × 30 seeds)

e. Strategy Assessment

The key constraint throughout: the data are bimodal, meaning the normality assumption is violated.

1. Standard ANOVA assuming normality — ❌ Not appropriate ANOVA requires (among other things) that residuals are approximately normally distributed within each group. A bimodal distribution is a direct violation of this assumption. Applying standard ANOVA without investigation would produce unreliable F-statistics and p-values.

2. Data transformation then ANOVA — ⚠️ Possibly appropriate, but risky Log or square-root transformations can correct right-skewness or stabilise variance, but they generally cannot resolve bimodality. Bimodality typically signals a mixture of two sub-populations (e.g., fast-germinating vs. slow-germinating seeds), and a transformation will not merge those modes into a single bell curve. This strategy should only be tried after investigating the source of bimodality; it is not a reliable fix.

3. Non-parametric test (e.g., Kruskal–Wallis) — ✅ Most appropriate The Kruskal–Wallis test is the non-parametric analogue of one-way ANOVA. It compares group medians/rank distributions without assuming normality, making it robust to bimodal or otherwise non-normal data. If it detects a significant difference, pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (with a correction such as Bonferroni) can follow.

# Example (conceptual — no data file provided for Ex. 1)
kruskal.test(germination_time ~ moisture_level, data = beans)

# Post-hoc pairwise comparisons
pairwise.wilcox.test(beans$germination_time, beans$moisture_level,
                     p.adjust.method = "bonferroni")

4. Ignore bimodality and use a t-test — ❌ Not appropriate A t-test has the same normality assumption as ANOVA, and ignoring the bimodality does not make it go away. Additionally, a t-test only compares two groups at a time; with three moisture levels, multiple t-tests inflate the Type I error rate (family-wise error). This strategy is doubly inappropriate.

5. Visualise with boxplots after choosing a test — ✅ Good practice, but note limitations Visualisation should ideally come before choosing a test, not after. That said, boxplots are always a valuable complement to any analysis. However, for bimodal data, boxplots alone can be misleading because they summarise the distribution with a single median and IQR, obscuring the two modes. Better visualisation choices for bimodal data include:

# Histogram per group
par(mfrow = c(1, 3))
for (lvl in levels(beans$moisture_level)) {
  hist(beans$germination_time[beans$moisture_level == lvl],
       main = paste("Moisture:", lvl),
       xlab = "Germination time (days)",
       col = "lightblue", breaks = 10)
}

# Or density plots overlaid
plot(density(beans$germination_time[beans$moisture_level == "Low"]),
     main = "Germination time by moisture level",
     xlab = "Days", col = "blue", lwd = 2)
lines(density(beans$germination_time[beans$moisture_level == "Medium"]),
      col = "red", lwd = 2)
lines(density(beans$germination_time[beans$moisture_level == "High"]),
      col = "green", lwd = 2)
legend("topright", legend = c("Low","Medium","High"),
       col = c("blue","red","green"), lwd = 2)

Kernel density plots or histograms make the bimodality visible and inform the choice of analysis.

  )

Comment: We expect that as industrial activity increases, water pH will deviate further from neutral — either becoming more acidic (lower pH) due to acid mine drainage or chemical discharge, or more alkaline due to certain industrial effluents. e. Gaussian adequacy for one industrial activity category

Selected category: High industrial activity (most interesting as deviations are expected to be largest)

# Subset: High industrial activity only
ph_high <- env_data$WaterpH[env_data$IndustrialActivity == levels(env_data$IndustrialActivity)[3]]
# (replace [3] with whichever level label corresponds to "High")

# --- Visual assessment ---
par(mfrow = c(1, 2))

# Histogram with normal curve overlay
hist(ph_high, freq = FALSE, breaks = 12,
     main = "pH — High Industrial Activity",
     xlab = "Water pH", col = "lightyellow",
     border = "grey40")
curve(dnorm(x, mean = mean(ph_high, na.rm = TRUE),
               sd   = sd(ph_high,   na.rm = TRUE)),
      add = TRUE, col = "red", lwd = 2)

# Q-Q plot
qqnorm(ph_high,
       main = "Q-Q Plot — pH (High Activity)",
       pch = 16, col = "steelblue")
qqline(ph_high, col = "red", lwd = 2)

par(mfrow = c(1, 1))

# --- Formal test ---
# Shapiro-Wilk (best for n < 50; use for each group separately)
shapiro.test(ph_high)

Comment: The Gaussian (normal) distribution is adequate if:

  • The histogram is roughly bell-shaped and symmetric around the mean.
  • The Q-Q plot points fall close to the diagonal reference line with no strong systematic curvature.
  • The Shapiro-Wilk test returns a p-value > 0.05 (we fail to reject normality).

In practice, for the high industrial activity group we might observe right- or left-skew in pH (because industrial effluents push values in a consistent direction), causing the Q-Q plot to curve at the tails. If the Shapiro-Wilk p-value is small (< 0.05), the Gaussian model is not adequate and we should consider a non-parametric approach or a transformation before applying parametric tests.

Exercise 3 — Mercury Contamination in Florida Lakes

Again, the data file was not uploaded. All code is complete and executable; outputs are described analytically.

a. Fit simple linear regression: Avg.Mercury ~ Alkalinity

# Load data (tab or space separated — check the file first)
mercury <- read.table("envdata-20250107-data2.txt", header = TRUE, sep = "\t")
# If space-separated: read.table(..., header = TRUE)

# Inspect
head(mercury)
str(mercury)

# Fit model
model1 <- lm(Avg.Mercury ~ Alkalinity, data = mercury)
summary(model1)

b. Model fit and interpretation

# Extract key statistics
s1 <- summary(model1)
cat("R-squared  :", round(s1$r.squared, 4), "\n")
cat("Slope      :", round(coef(model1)[2], 5), "\n")
cat("Intercept  :", round(coef(model1)[1], 4), "\n")
cat("p-value (slope):", round(s1$coefficients[2, 4], 5), "\n")

Interpretation:

  • Intercept: The estimated average mercury concentration when alkalinity = 0 mg/L. This is rarely physically meaningful on its own (lakes have some minimum alkalinity), but anchors the regression line.
  • Slope: For each 1 mg/L increase in alkalinity, average mercury concentration changes by the estimated slope value (expected to be negative — more alkaline lakes tend to have lower mercury bioavailability). For example, a slope of −0.01 means a 10 mg/L rise in alkalinity is associated with a 0.10 mg/L decrease in average mercury.
  • R²: Proportion of variance in mercury concentration explained by alkalinity alone. A value of, say, 0.35 means alkalinity explains 35% of the variability — modest but meaningful for a simple one-predictor model.
  • p-value of slope: If < 0.05, alkalinity is a statistically significant predictor.

c. Potential problems — residual diagnostics

# Residual diagnostic plots (base R — 4-panel)
par(mfrow = c(2, 2))
plot(model1)
par(mfrow = c(1, 1))

What to look for and expected problems:

Plot What to look for Likely issue
Residuals vs Fitted Random scatter around 0 Likely a curved (non-linear) pattern — suggests the linear model misses curvature
Q-Q plot of residuals Points on the diagonal Right-skewed residuals (mercury concentrations are bounded at 0 and right-skewed)
Scale-Location Horizontal band Heteroscedasticity — variance of residuals likely increases with fitted values
Residuals vs Leverage No points beyond Cook’s distance Possible influential lakes with extreme alkalinity

The key problems are: (1) non-linearity (the relationship may be better described on a log scale), (2) non-normality of residuals (right skew), and (3) heteroscedasticity.

d. Log-transformation to improve the model

# Create log-transformed variables
mercury$log_Hg  <- log(mercury$Avg.Mercury)
mercury$log_Alk <- log(mercury$Alkalinity)

# Model 2: log(Y) ~ X  (log-response only)
model2 <- lm(log_Hg ~ Alkalinity, data = mercury)

# Model 3: log(Y) ~ log(X)  (log-log / power model)
model3 <- lm(log_Hg ~ log_Alk, data = mercury)

# Model 4: Y ~ log(X)  (log-predictor only)
model4 <- lm(Avg.Mercury ~ log_Alk, data = mercury)

# Compare summaries
summary(model2)
summary(model3)
summary(model4)

# Compare residual diagnostics
par(mfrow = c(2, 2)); plot(model2); par(mfrow = c(1, 1))
par(mfrow = c(2, 2)); plot(model3); par(mfrow = c(1, 1))

e. Best-fitting model discussion

# Formal comparison of R² across models
cat("Model 1 (Y ~ X)          R² =", round(summary(model1)$r.squared, 4), "\n")
cat("Model 2 (logY ~ X)       R² =", round(summary(model2)$r.squared, 4), "\n")
cat("Model 3 (logY ~ logX)    R² =", round(summary(model3)$r.squared, 4), "\n")
cat("Model 4 (Y ~ logX)       R² =", round(summary(model4)$r.squared, 4), "\n")

# AIC comparison (lower = better; penalises complexity)
AIC(model1, model2, model3, model4)

Expected best model: log(Avg.Mercury) ~ log(Alkalinity) — the log-log model (Model 3).

Why: - Mercury concentrations are strictly positive and right-skewed — logging the response stabilises variance and symmetrises residuals. - Alkalinity spans a wide positive range — logging the predictor linearises the diminishing-returns relationship. - The log-log specification corresponds to a power-law model: Mercury = a · Alkalinity^b, which is ecologically interpretable. - It typically gives the highest R², lowest AIC, and most homoscedastic, normally distributed residuals of the four models.

f. Slope interpretation and 95% CI for best model (Model 3)

# Coefficient estimates and 95% CI
coef(model3)
confint(model3, level = 0.95)

# Full summary
summary(model3)

Interpretation of slope in the log-log model:

In the model log(Avg.Mercury) = β₀ + β₁ · log(Alkalinity):

  • β₁ is an elasticity: a 1% increase in alkalinity is associated with a β₁% change in average mercury concentration.
  • Since the relationship is expected to be negative (higher alkalinity → lower mercury), β₁ < 0. For example, β₁ = −0.5 means a 10% increase in alkalinity is associated with approximately a 5% decrease in average mercury.
  • The 95% confidence interval for β₁ (from confint) gives the range of plausible values for this elasticity. If the entire interval is negative (does not cross zero), we have strong evidence that alkalinity is inversely associated with mercury contamination, consistent with the study’s findings.

new_alk <- data.frame(log_Alk = log(50))  # e.g., alkalinity = 50 mg/L
pred_log_hg <- predict(model3, newdata = new_alk, interval = "confidence")
exp(pred_log_hg)  # back-transform to original mercury scale
